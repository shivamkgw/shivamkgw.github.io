The Infallibility Trap: Why Indian Leadership Needs to Unlearn the Fear of Making Mistakes

"Log kya kahenge" isn't just a punchline about weddings and career choices. It's the operating system running underneath most Indian workplaces and families, and it is quietly making them worse at their jobs.

Division 1

Why can't anyone just say "I was wrong"?

The Hook

Sit in enough meetings across enough Indian organizations and you start noticing a pattern. A project misses its numbers. Everyone in the room already knows why. And yet the conversation spends forty-five minutes circling the actual cause, because naming it out loud would mean someone senior has to own it. So instead you get a "market conditions" explanation, a vague action plan nobody will follow, and the exact same mistake showing up again next quarter.

This isn't a story about individual bad leaders. It is a structural feature of how hierarchy works in much of Indian professional and family life. The people at the top of a steep hierarchy are under enormous, mostly invisible pressure to appear infallible, and the people underneath them learn fast that pointing out an error is a career risk, not a contribution.

The result is an organization that is very good at appearing correct and quite bad at actually correcting itself. That gap is the subject of this piece: where it comes from, what it costs, and the specific mechanical changes that close it, because "just be more open to feedback" has never once worked as advice.

The cover-up is never really about the original mistake. It's about what admitting the mistake would say about the person's right to be in charge.

Division 2

The science behind the ego

The Why

It's tempting to write this off as "Indians have big egos," but that explains nothing and lets everyone off the hook. The more useful question is why this specific defensiveness exists, and there's a real answer rooted in how power is structured, not just how people happen to behave.

Start with Hofstede's Power Distance Index. Geert Hofstede's cross-cultural research puts a number on this. His Power Distance Index measures how much the people lower down a hierarchy simply accept that power should be unequal, that the boss deserves to be obeyed, not just followed. India scores 77 out of 100 on this measure, well above the global average of roughly 55. That's not a score for how bosses behave; it's a score for how much everyone underneath them already expects and accepts the gap. When a hierarchy is that steep, the person at the top isn't just senior, they're expected to be a different category of person altogether, which makes an admitted mistake feel less like an event and more like a demotion in kind.

There's a second, older lens worth bringing in here, carefully: the distinction anthropologist Ruth Benedict drew in 1946 between guilt cultures, where wrongdoing is policed by an internalized conscience, and shame cultures, where it's policed by the fear of public exposure. It's a genuinely useful heuristic for why "saving face" can override honesty even when no one is punishing you for the original mistake, only for admitting it. It's worth saying plainly, though, that this framework is a classic and contested one, not settled anthropology. Benedict wrote about Japan without doing fieldwork there, and critics have rightly pushed back on treating any culture as a monolith. Use it as a lens, not a law: it explains a mechanism, it doesn't prove a national character.

Put those two ideas together and you get a coherent picture. A steep power gradient raises the stakes of being seen as fallible. A shame-oriented instinct for saving face supplies the reflex to hide the error rather than confess it. Neither one requires anyone to be a bad person. Both are just what the incentives produce.

Division 3

What zero psychological safety actually costs

The Research

Harvard Business School's Amy Edmondson gave this problem a name that has since become standard in organizational research: psychological safety, a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking, in practice a climate where people trust they won't be punished or humiliated for speaking up with a question, a concern, or a mistake. It's worth being precise about what this is not: it isn't "niceness," and it isn't the absence of accountability. It's the specific, narrow condition that has to exist before anyone will tell you the truth about something that's already gone wrong.

This isn't just a plausible-sounding connection between hierarchy and secrecy, either. Researchers studied 106 medical residents and found a clear chain: the more juniors felt the power gap between themselves and their seniors, the less psychologically safe they felt. And the less safe they felt, the less likely they were to actually report their own medical errors. Separate research on whistleblowing found the same pattern in a completely different setting: in high power-distance workplaces, people are simply less likely to report wrongdoing when they see it. High hierarchy doesn't just make people uncomfortable disagreeing with the boss. It measurably suppresses the reporting of real, consequential errors.

Psychological safety isn't the absence of standards. It's the precondition for anyone telling you the truth before a small problem becomes an expensive one.

None of these studies were run specifically on Indian organizations, and it would overstate the evidence to claim otherwise. But the condition they do measure, a steep gap in power between seniors and juniors, is exactly what India scores unusually high on. Nobody has run this exact study in an Indian company yet. But the pattern researchers found elsewhere lines up precisely with the conditions India already has.

Division 4

The hidden cost

The Impact

None of this stays theoretical for long. Once admitting a mistake becomes more dangerous than the mistake itself, three specific failure modes show up, reliably, in that order.

Where the Cost Shows Up

  1. The cover-up outgrows the crime A minor, cheap-to-fix error gets patched over instead of corrected, because a real fix would require someone to name what went wrong. Small problems compound quietly until they surface as expensive, public failures, at which point there's no longer a version of events where nobody looks bad.
  2. The leader becomes an echo chamber of one Once it's clear that disagreeing with the boss carries a cost, people stop doing it. Not dramatically, just quietly, meeting by meeting. The leader keeps hearing that the plan is working right up until it visibly isn't, because nobody wanted to be the one who said otherwise first.
  3. Everyone pays a permanent tax in stress Maintaining the appearance of infallibility is exhausting, for the leader defending it and for the team managing around it. That's not a metaphor; it's ongoing cognitive and emotional load, and it's a well-documented driver of burnout in high-hierarchy, low-safety environments.

The uncomfortable part is that all three of these are more expensive, in money and in trust, than the original mistake ever was. The culture optimizes for looking right in the room today at the cost of being right next quarter.

Division 5

How to actually fix it

The Action Plan

A motivational poster about "embracing failure" changes nothing, because the problem was never a lack of awareness. It's an incentive structure, and incentive structures only change when the mechanics change. Here's what actually moves the needle, in order of how much power you need to implement it.

  • Leaders bleed first Culture flows downward, not upward. You cannot ask a junior employee to admit an error if the person running the meeting has never once done it themselves. The single most effective intervention available to anyone with authority is standing up and saying, plainly, "I made the wrong call here, this is why my reasoning failed, and this is what I'm doing differently." When the person with the most power normalizes being wrong, everyone below them gets permission to be honest too.
  • Attack the process, not the person In a shame-oriented culture, an error reads as a character flaw by default, so the first question after anything goes wrong ("who did this?") turns every mistake into a trial. Replace it with a different first question: "what broke in our process that let this happen?" That single substitution separates a person's identity from an operational failure, and it's the difference between a team that hides problems and one that reports them.
  • Build a release valve before you ask for openness Teams that have spent years in a high-power-distance environment will not walk into a room and start openly contradicting a senior person, no matter how sincerely you invite it. Anonymous feedback channels aren't a permanent solution, but they're a real stepping stone: a way to surface a problem without anyone having to be the first person seen doing something culturally risky. Once people see that flagged issues get fixed and nobody gets punished, you can start dialing down the anonymity.
  • Redefine what respect means The underlying belief that has to change is the definition of respect itself. In a steep hierarchy, respect usually means never questioning the person above you. It has to be redefined as caring enough about the outcome to tell them the truth, especially when it's inconvenient. That redefinition is the whole project. Everything else on this list is just a mechanism for making it survivable to practice.

None of this requires a personality transplant, for you or for anyone you work with. It requires changing what gets rewarded when something goes wrong, consistently, until the old reflex to hide it stops being the rational choice.
